Question 1 — 2024 AMC 8 Problem 25Introductory combinatorics

A small airplane has 4 rows of seats with 3 seats in each row. Eight passengers have boarded the plane and are distributed randomly among the seats. A married couple is next to board. What is the probability there will be 2 adjacent seats in the same row for the couple?

  • (A)8/15
  • (B)32/55
  • (C)20/33
  • (D)34/55
  • (E)8/11
  1. Track the empty seats, not the passengers. There are 12 seats and 8 passengers, so exactly 4 seats stay empty, and every set of 4 empty seats is equally likely.
    C(12,4) = 495 equally likely outcomes
  2. Translate the condition. A row of 3 has two adjacent pairs: seats 1–2 and seats 2–3. The couple can sit together exactly when some row has two empty seats that are next to each other.
  3. Count the complement — no row contains two adjacent empty seats. Within a single row, the legal empty-sets are: none (1 way), any one seat (3 ways), or the two outer seats (1 way). Three empties in a row is never legal.
    Empties in a row0123
    Legal patterns1310
  4. Spread 4 empties over 4 rows using only those legal patterns, splitting into cases by how many rows hold 2, 1, or 0 empty seats.
    2+2+0+0 : C(4,2) × 1 × 1 = 6
    2+1+1+0 : 4 × C(3,2) × 1 × 3 × 3 = 108
    1+1+1+1 : 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81
    total = 6 + 108 + 81 = 195
  5. Subtract from 1.
    1 − 195/495 = 300/495 = 20/33
Answer(C) 20/33

Counting the complement is the move here. Counting "at least one good row" directly forces you into inclusion–exclusion over four overlapping rows.

Question 2 — 2019 AMC 8 Problem 19Introductory combinatorics

In a tournament there are six teams that play each other twice. A team earns 3 points for a win, 1 point for a draw, and 0 points for a loss. After all the games have been played it turns out that the top three teams earned the same number of total points. What is the greatest possible number of total points for each of the top three teams?

  • (A)22
  • (B)23
  • (C)24
  • (D)26
  • (E)30
  1. Bound the points pool of the top three, then divide by 3. Sort the games into two kinds: those played inside the top three, and those against the bottom three.
  2. Games inside the top three. Three pairs, played twice each, is 6 games. Each game hands out 3 points if it is decisive and only 2 if it is drawn, so this bucket contributes at most
    6 × 3 = 18 points
  3. Games against the bottom three. Each of the 3 top teams plays each of the 3 bottom teams twice: 3 × 3 × 2 = 18 games. At best the top teams win them all.
    18 × 3 = 54 points
  4. Divide the ceiling three ways.
    (18 + 54) ÷ 3 = 72 ÷ 3 = 24 points each
  5. Show 24 is reachable. Let the top three sweep all 6 of their games against the bottom three — that is 18 points apiece. Inside the top three, run a cycle: A beats B twice, B beats C twice, C beats A twice. Each team goes 2–2 there for 6 more points.
    18 + 6 = 24 for each of A, B, C — a three-way tie
Answer(C) 24

The cycle matters. If the top three drew with each other instead, the internal bucket would drop to 12 points and the tie would only reach 22.

Question 3 — 2026 AMC 8 Problem 25Introductory combinatorics

In an equiangular hexagon, all interior angles measure 120°. An example of such a hexagon with side lengths 2, 3, 1, 3, 2, and 2 is shown below, inscribed in equilateral triangle ABC. Consider all equiangular hexagons with positive integer side lengths that can be inscribed in △ABC, with all six vertices on the sides of the triangle. What is the total number of such hexagons? Hexagons that differ only by a rotation or a reflection are considered the same.

  • (A)4
  • (B)5
  • (C)6
  • (D)7
  • (E)8
  1. Describe the hexagon by the three corners you cut off. Because every angle is 120°, each corner cut is itself an equilateral triangle. Call the cut sizes x, y, z at corners A, B, C. The hexagon's six sides, in order, are
    x, s−x−y, y, s−y−z, z, s−z−x   (s = side of △ABC)
  2. Find s from the example. Its cuts are 1, 2, 2, and each side of the triangle is a cut, a hexagon side, then another cut.
    s = 1 + 3 + 2 = 6
  3. Turn the conditions into inequalities. All six side lengths must be positive integers, so x, y, z ≥ 1 and each remaining piece is at least 1:
    x + y ≤ 5,  y + z ≤ 5,  z + x ≤ 5
  4. Handle the symmetry. Rotating the hexagon cycles (x, y, z) and reflecting it reverses the order, so two triples give the same hexagon exactly when they are the same multiset. Count unordered triples with x ≤ y ≤ z.
  5. Count by how many cut sizes are equal.

    All three equal: (1, 1, 1) and (2, 2, 2) work. The next possibility, (3, 3, 3), is degenerate: three sides collapse to length 0, leaving a triangle.

    Exactly two equal: the possibilities are (1, 1, 2), (1, 1, 3), (1, 1, 4), (1, 2, 2), and (2, 2, 3).

    All three different: the only possibility is (1, 2, 3).

    2 + 5 + 1 = 8
Answer(E) 8

{2,2,2} is the regular hexagon with all sides 2, and {1,1,4} gives sides 1, 4, 1, 1, 4, 1 — the flattest one that still fits.

Question 4 — 2014 AMC 8 Problem 12Introductory combinatorics

A magazine printed photos of three celebrities along with three photos of the celebrities as babies. The baby pictures did not identify the celebrities. Readers were asked to match each celebrity with the correct baby picture. What is the probability that a reader guessing at random will match all three correctly?

  • (A)1/9
  • (B)1/6
  • (C)1/4
  • (D)1/3
  • (E)1/2
  1. A guess is an assignment of the three baby photos to the three celebrities — that is, an ordering of the three photos.
    3! = 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 possible guesses
  2. Exactly one of those six is completely right, and a random guesser picks each equally often.
    1 ÷ 6 = 1/6
Answer(B) 1/6

Sequential version, same result: 1/3 chance on the first celebrity, then 1/2 on the second, and the third is forced. (1/3)(1/2)(1) = 1/6.

Question 5 — 2022 AMC 8 Problem 23Introductory combinatorics

A △ or ○ is placed in each of the nine squares in a 3-by-3 grid. How many configurations will have three △s in a line and three ○s in a line?

  • (A)39
  • (B)42
  • (C)78
  • (D)84
  • (E)96
Solution 1

Count pairs of lines, then repair the overcount

  1. Find the pairs of lines that can carry the two colours. Call a row, column, or diagonal solid when all 3 of its squares contain the same symbol. A valid pair consists of one solid △-line and one solid ○-line. The two lines must be disjoint, since a shared square cannot hold both symbols. There are 8 lines in total: 3 rows, 3 columns, and 2 diagonals.
  2. Only parallel rows or parallel columns are disjoint. A row and a column always cross; every diagonal crosses every row, every column, and the other diagonal.
    C(3,2) rows + C(3,2) columns = 3 + 3 = 6 unordered pairs
  3. Assign symbols and fill the rest. For each pair, either line can be the △-line, and each of the 3 leftover squares can independently be △ or ○.
    6 × 2 × 2³ = 12 × 8 = 96
    This calculation counts choices of valid pairs, not distinct completed grids. Therefore, a grid is counted once for every valid pair it contains.
  4. See where double counting occurs. Suppose all 3 rows are solid and both symbols appear:
    R₁: △ △ △
    R₂: △ △ △
    R₃: ○ ○ ○
    This grid contains two valid pairs: (R₁, R₃) and (R₂, R₃). The ○-row can pair with either △-row, so the same completed grid appears twice in the count of 96.
  5. Count the double-counted row-grids. If all 3 rows are solid and both symbols appear, one symbol must occupy 2 rows and the other must occupy 1 row. The minority-symbol row forms a valid pair with each of the two majority-symbol rows, so every such grid contains exactly two valid pairs.

    Each solid row independently has 2 choices: all △ or all ○. This gives 2³ = 8 row patterns. Exclude the all-△ and all-○ patterns because they do not contain both symbols.

    Symbols on R₁, R₂, R₃Both present?
    △ △ △No
    △ △ ○Yes
    △ ○ △Yes
    ○ △ △Yes
    △ ○ ○Yes
    ○ △ ○Yes
    ○ ○ △Yes
    ○ ○ ○No
    2³ − 2 = 8 − 2 = 6 double-counted row-grids
  6. Subtract only the extra copies. The same reasoning gives 6 double-counted column-grids. Each exceptional grid was counted twice but should be counted once, so subtract one extra copy of each—not both copies. It is like counting a student once for each club they belong to: a student in two clubs needs one duplicate removed.
    96 − 6 − 6 = 84
Answer(D) 84

No other grid is double counted: a solid row of △ and a solid column of ○ would clash where they meet, so mixed row-and-column overcounts cannot happen.

Solution 2

Build the grids directly, so nothing is counted twice

Solution 1 counts pairs of lines and then repairs the overcount. This version counts finished grids instead, so no correction is needed at the end.

  1. The two solid lines can never cross. If the △-line and the ○-line shared a square, that square would have to hold both symbols. So the two lines are disjoint — which, among the 8 lines, leaves only two parallel rows or two parallel columns. A row and a column always meet, and each diagonal meets every row, every column, and the other diagonal.
  2. A grid is a "row grid" or a "column grid," never both. If one row is all △ and another row is all ○, then every column contains one of each symbol, so no column can be solid. The two families never overlap, so we can count them separately and add.
  3. Count the row grids. Split on whether the third row is solid too.
    • Case A — the leftover row is mixed Choose the all-△ row (3 ways), then the all-○ row from the two that remain (2 ways). The last row can be any pattern that uses both symbols: 2³ − 2 = 6.
      3 × 2 × 6 = 36
    • Case B — all three rows are solid Both symbols must appear, so one symbol fills two rows and the other fills one. Choose the odd row out (3 ways) and the symbol on the doubled pair (2 ways).
      3 × 2 = 6
    36 + 6 = 42 row grids
  4. Columns behave identically, giving another 42. By step 2 these two lists share nothing, so add them.
    42 + 42 = 84
Same answer(D) 84

The Case B grids are exactly the six Solution 1 had to subtract: they contain two valid pairs of lines, but here each one is built exactly once, because a grid is described by its actual pattern of rows rather than by a pair of lines inside it.
