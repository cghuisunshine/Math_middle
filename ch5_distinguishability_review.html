Section 5.4

Distinguishability

Distinguishable means we can tell the items apart; indistinguishable means we can't. There are 3! = 6 ways to order 3 distinguishable items, but only 1 way to order 3 indistinguishable ones — all the orders look the same.

Take 4 balls and 2 boxes. Either the balls or the boxes can be distinguishable or not, so the same sentence — "put the balls in the boxes" — is really four different problems with four different answers.

Problem 5.6

How many ways are there to put 4 distinguishable balls into 2 distinguishable boxes?

For each ball there are 2 choices of box, and the choice is independent for each of the 4 balls, so multiply:

2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 2⁴ = 16

16

Problem 5.7

How many ways are there to put 4 distinguishable balls into 2 indistinguishable boxes?

Now we don't care which box is which. We only care which balls are together and which aren't. Two routes:

Route 1 — divide out the box labels

Take the 16 from Problem 5.6. There are 2! = 2 ways to arrange the 2 boxes, so

16 ÷ 2 = 8
Warning

This shortcut does not generalise to more than 2 boxes — see Exercise 5.4.2(e). It is exact here because no labelled arrangement survives a box-swap unchanged: that would require the two boxes to hold identical contents, which is impossible when the balls are all different. So the 16 labelled pictures pair up perfectly, 2 to 1.

Route 2 — casework on the shape

  • 4 + 0. All four balls together: 1 way (it doesn't matter which box, they're identical).
  • 3 + 1. Choose the lone ball: 4 ways.
  • 2 + 2. Choosing 2 balls for "the first box" gives (42) = 6, but picking {A,B} and picking {C,D} produce the same picture. Divide by 2: 3 ways.
1 + 4 + 3 = 8

8   Two methods, same answer — that's the check.

Problem 5.8

How many ways are there to put 4 indistinguishable balls into 2 distinguishable boxes?

Since the balls are identical, the only thing worth tracking is how many balls are in each box. Box #1 can hold 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4 balls, and the rest go to box #2.

(0,4) (1,3) (2,2) (3,1) (4,0) → 5

5

Problem 5.9

How many ways are there to put 4 indistinguishable balls into 2 indistinguishable boxes?

Nothing is labelled, so all that survives is the split — the multiset of counts. There are only three ways to split 4 identical items into two groups:

{4, 0}   {3, 1}   {2, 2}

3

Important

Distinguishability matters. There's nothing to memorise here. On any counting problem, ask what you can and can't tell apart, then use logic. The point of Problems 5.6–5.9 is to make it unmistakable that there is a difference, and that you have to account for it.

Here's the thing worth being careful about. In Problem 5.7 the 2+2 case needed an extra division by 2, so it's tempting to think the even 3+3 split breaks the overall "divide by 2!" shortcut here. It doesn't:

The shortcut fails only when some labelled arrangement is left unchanged by swapping the boxes — which would need both boxes to hold the same balls, impossible when the balls are all different. Practice 5 ★ — partly distinguishable

Three balls are indistinguishably red and two are indistinguishably blue. How many ways are there to put all five into 3 distinguishable boxes?

Modelled on Problem 5.16(e)

The reds and the blues never interfere — knowing where the reds went tells you nothing about the blues — so place each colour independently and multiply.

  • 3 identical reds into 3 labelled boxes: C(3+2, 2) = C(5,2) = 10
  • 2 identical blues into 3 labelled boxes: C(2+2, 2) = C(4,2) = 6
10 × 6 = 60

60

Sanity check against the extremes: if all five balls were identical you'd get C(7,2) = 21, and if all five were different you'd get 3⁵ = 243. Partial distinguishability lands in between, as it must.

(e)★ Why 243 ÷ 3! is wrong

First, the fatal clue: 243 ÷ 6 = 40.5, which isn't even a whole number. So the method can't be right.

Dividing by 3! assumes every unlabelled picture comes from exactly 6 labelled ones. That's only true when all three boxes hold different contents. If two boxes hold the same thing — including two empty boxes — swapping them changes nothing, so that picture is produced by fewer than 6 labelled arrangements.

Example. All 5 balls in one box. There are only 3 labelled versions of this (balls in box 1, box 2, or box 3), not 6, because the two empty boxes are interchangeable.

The division trick works only when no arrangement is fixed by any box-swap. With 2 boxes and an odd number of balls that's guaranteed; with 3 boxes it fails immediately.

Section 5.5

Summary

Grid paths

Combinations count paths on a grid. If a path needs a total steps in one direction and b total steps in another, and the steps can come in any order, there are

C(a+b, a) = C(a+b, b)   possible paths.

Make sure you understand why: a path is just a word made of a R's and b U's, and choosing the path means choosing which of the a+b positions hold the R's.

Combine your tools

Complementary counting and casework don't go away once you learn combinations — the harder committee-forming problems need all three together.

Distinguishability matters

Keep track of whether the objects are distinguishable or indistinguishable, and proceed accordingly.

Concept

If it's easy to do, solving a problem in two different ways is a good way to check your answer.

"All man's troubles come from not knowing how to sit still in one room." — Blaise Pascal

Problem 5.10

In the diagram below (paths only go up and to the right):

  • (a)How many paths are there from A to B?
  • (b)How many paths are there from A to C?
  • (c)How many paths are there from C to B?
  • (d)How many paths are there from A to B passing through C?

Put A at the origin. The grid is 5 across and 4 up, so B = (5, 4). The point C is one step right and two steps up: C = (1, 2).

(a) A → B

Every path is 5 R's and 4 U's in some order — 9 steps, choose which 4 are U.

C(9, 4) = 126

126

(b) A → C

1 right, 2 up: 3 steps, choose which 1 is R.

C(3, 1) = 3

3

(c) C → B

From (1,2) to (5,4) is 4 right and 2 up: 6 steps, choose which 2 are U.

C(6, 2) = 15

(d) A → B through C

A path through C splits into an independent first leg and second leg, so multiply.

3 × 15 = 45

Problem 5.13

Zichen has 6 different T-shirts and 8 different polo shirts. He needs to select 6 shirts to pack for an upcoming vacation. In how many ways can Zichen select 6 shirts

  • (a)without restrictions?
  • (b)to have 3 T-shirts and 3 polo shirts?
  • (c)to have more polo shirts than T-shirts?

(a)

All 14 shirts are different; just choose 6 of them.

C(14, 6) = 3003

3003

(b)

Choose the T-shirts and the polos independently.

C(6,3) × C(8,3) = 20 × 56 = 1120

1120

(c)

With 6 shirts total, "more polos than T-shirts" means at least 4 polos. Casework:

  • 4 polos, 2 tees: C(8,4) × C(6,2) = 70 × 15 = 1050
  • 5 polos, 1 tee: C(8,5) × C(6,1) = 56 × 6 = 336
  • 6 polos, 0 tees: C(8,6) × C(6,0) = 28 × 1 = 28
1050 + 336 + 28 = 1414

1414

Problem 5.14

The Sagebrush High School student council has 6 juniors and 6 seniors as class representatives. Two committees, each consisting of 2 juniors and 2 seniors, are to be created. If no student can serve on both committees, how many different pairs of committees are possible?

Source: MATHCOUNTS

Build the committees one at a time, then correct for the fact that the two committees aren't labelled.

  • First committee: C(6,2) juniors × C(6,2) seniors = 15 × 15 = 225
  • Second committee, from who's left: C(4,2) × C(4,2) = 6 × 6 = 36
225 × 36 = 8100 ordered pairs

But a pair of committees is unordered — calling one "first" and the other "second" counted each pair exactly twice. The two committees can never be identical (no shared students), so the division is clean.

8100 ÷ 2 = 4050

4050

Problem 5.15

Our basketball team has 10 players, including Steve and Danny. We need to divide into two teams of 5 for an intra-squad scrimmage. In how many ways can we do this:

  • (a)without restrictions?
  • (b)if Steve and Danny insist on playing on the same team?
  • (c)if Steve and Danny insist on playing on opposing teams?
  • (d)How are your answers to (a), (b) and (c) related?

(a)

Choosing 5 players picks out both teams at once, and choosing a set of 5 or its complement gives the same division — the teams are unlabelled.

C(10,5) ÷ 2 = 252 ÷ 2 = 126

126

(b) same team

Put Steve and Danny together, then fill their team with 3 of the other 8. That determines everything, and there's no double-count left to fix (the pair is on one specific side).

C(8, 3) = 56

56

(c) opposing teams

Steve's team needs 4 more players from the remaining 8; Danny's team gets the rest.

C(8, 4) = 70

70

(d) the relationship

Steve and Danny are either together or apart — there is no third option — so (b) and (c) partition (a):

56 + 70 = 126

That's a complementary-counting check on all three answers at once.

Problem 5.17

In the diagram below, the grid path from F to G is missing, so paths from D to E cannot pass between F and G. How many paths are there from D to E? (All steps go up or to the right.)

Put D at the origin. The grid is 5 across and 3 up, so E = (5, 3), F = (2, 2) and G = (3, 2).

Route 1 — complementary counting

Count all paths, then subtract the ones that use the forbidden segment.

All paths D → E: C(8, 3) = 56

A path uses the F→G segment exactly when it goes D → F, crosses, then G → E. Those legs are independent:

  • D(0,0) → F(2,2): 2 right and 2 up — C(4,2) = 6
  • G(3,2) → E(5,3): 2 right and 1 up — C(3,1) = 3
Bad paths: 6 × 3 = 18  →  56 − 18 = 38

Route 2 — count forward on the lattice

Write 1 at D and give every other point the sum of the point below it and the point to its left — skipping the missing edge. Notice G gets 4 instead of 10, because nothing can reach it from F.

38   Both methods agree.

Problem 5.18

Nine lines are drawn in a plane. What is the largest possible number of points in the plane at which at least two of the nine lines intersect?

Two distinct lines meet in at most one point, so each intersection point is "caused by" at least one pair of lines. To maximise the count we want every pair to meet, and no two pairs to meet at the same place — that is, no two lines parallel and no three lines concurrent. Both conditions are easy to arrange (take nine lines in general position).

C(9, 2) = 36

36

Problem 5.19

Three goats and seven llamas are to be split into two 5-animal groups, such that each group has at least one goat and at least one llama. In how many ways can this be accomplished?

The two groups are unlabelled, so work with ordered groups and halve at the end.

Say group 1 gets g goats; then it gets 5 − g llamas, and group 2 gets 3 − g goats and 2 + g llamas. The conditions:

  • g ≥ 1 and 3 − g ≥ 1 → g = 1 or 2
  • 5 − g ≥ 1 and 2 + g ≥ 1 → automatic

So only two cases survive, and a group can never be all-goats (only 3 goats exist for a group of 5) or all-llamas without breaking the goat condition.

  • g = 1: C(3,1) × C(7,4) = 3 × 35 = 105
  • g = 2: C(3,2) × C(7,3) = 3 × 35 = 105
(105 + 105) ÷ 2 = 105

The two groups are never identical (they'd need the same goat count, impossible with 3 goats), so halving is exact.

105

Check against the unrestricted count: C(10,5) ÷ 2 = 126 total splits, and the only forbidden ones put all 3 goats in one group (that group is 3 goats + 2 llamas, leaving the other group all llamas) — C(7,2) = 21 of them. 126 − 21 = 105. ✓

Problem 5.21

How many positive integers less than 1000 are there such that the digits are not in strictly increasing order from left to right? (The digits of 124 are in strictly increasing order, but the digits of 88 and 946 are not.)

"Not" in the problem statement is the signal for complementary counting. There are 999 positive integers less than 1000. Count the strictly increasing ones and subtract.

Key observation: if you pick any set of distinct nonzero digits, there is exactly one way to arrange them in increasing order. And 0 can never appear — it would have to come first, which isn't allowed for a leading digit.

  • 1-digit: any of 1–9, all trivially increasing — C(9,1) = 9
  • 2-digit: C(9,2) = 36
  • 3-digit: C(9,3) = 84
9 + 36 + 84 = 129 increasing  →  999 − 129 = 870

870

Problem 5.22

A party consists of twenty mathematicians and twenty physicists. Every mathematician shakes hands with every other person at the party. Half of the physicists only shake hands with mathematicians; the other half of the physicists all also shake hands with each other. How many handshakes in total are there?

Sort the handshakes by who's involved — three disjoint types.

  • Mathematician–mathematician: every pair shakes — C(20,2) = 190
  • Mathematician–physicist: every mathematician shakes with every physicist — 20 × 20 = 400
  • Physicist–physicist: only among the sociable half, 10 of them — C(10,2) = 45
190 + 400 + 45 = 635

635

The trap is counting a handshake twice. Fixing the types first keeps the cases disjoint, so no correction is needed.

Problem 5.23

Five different circles are drawn in a plane. What is the maximum number of different points at which the circles can meet?

Two distinct circles meet in at most 2 points — the key difference from Problem 5.18, where two lines met in at most 1. With five circles arranged so that every pair meets twice and no point is shared by three circles:

2 × C(5, 2) = 2 × 10 = 20

20

Problem 5.24 ★

I have n friends. Every night of the 365-day year I invite three of them to dinner. What is the smallest n could be such that it is still possible for me to make these invitations without ever inviting the same group of three friends? (I can invite the same friend, or even the same pair of friends, but never the same whole group of three.)

Each night uses up one 3-element subset of my n friends, and no subset may repeat. So I need at least 365 subsets available:

C(n, 3) ≥ 365

Test values:

  • C(13, 3) = 286 — not enough
  • C(14, 3) = 364 — one night short!
  • C(15, 3) = 455 — enough

The near-miss at 14 is the whole point of the problem.

n = 15

Problem 5.25 ★

An integer is called snakelike if its decimal representation a₁a₂a₃…ak satisfies ai < ai+1 when i is odd and ai > ai+1 when i is even. How many snakelike integers between 1000 and 9999 have four distinct digits?

Source: AIME

For a 4-digit number a₁a₂a₃a₄ the conditions are a₁ < a₂ > a₃ < a₄ — an up-down-up zigzag.

Strategy: first choose the set of four distinct digits, then count how many orderings zigzag. With four distinct values w < x < y < z, exactly 5 of the 24 orderings are up-down-up:

wyxz · wzxy · xywz · xzwy · yzwx

(Check one: xywz means a₁=x, a₂=y, a₃=w, a₄=z, and indeed x < y > w < z. ✓  The largest value z can never sit in position 1 or 3, which is what cuts 24 down to 5.)

Case 1 — 0 is not one of the digits

Choose 4 digits from 1–9, and all 5 orderings are legal.

C(9, 4) × 5 = 126 × 5 = 630

Case 2 — 0 is one of the digits

Choose the other 3 digits from 1–9: C(9,3) = 84 sets. Now 0 is the smallest value w, and it may not lead. Of the five orderings, the two beginning with w (wyxz and wzxy) are out, leaving 3.

C(9, 3) × 3 = 84 × 3 = 252 630 + 252 = 882

882

Problem 5.26 ★

Mack the bug starts at the point (0,0,0) at noon and each minute moves one unit in either the positive x-direction, the positive y-direction, or the positive z-direction. How many different paths could he take to (3,5,2)?

This is the grid-path idea in three dimensions. Mack must make 3 x-steps, 5 y-steps and 2 z-steps — 10 steps total — and the path is entirely determined by the order of those steps. So a path is a rearrangement of the word

X X X Y Y Y Y Y Z Z

Choose which 3 of the 10 positions are X, then which 5 of the remaining 7 are Y:

C(10,3) × C(7,5) = 120 × 21 = 2520

Equivalently, the multinomial:

10! / (3! · 5! · 2!) = 3628800 / 1440 = 2520

2520

Extra!

Catalan numbers

Counting paths across a rectangular grid gives combinations. What happens if we take only half the grid?

Consider the lower half of an n × n square grid — everything on or below the diagonal from the lower-left corner A to the upper-right corner B. Count the paths from A to B moving only up or to the right, with no cheating along the diagonal segments.

The reflection trick

On a full 5 × 5 grid there are 10 steps — 5 up and 5 right — so C(10,5) paths; in general C(2n, n). But the diagonal restriction rules some of them out. A path like "rruuurruur" strays above the diagonal and is illegal.

Here's the clever part. Take any illegal path, find the first point where it strays above the diagonal, and flip everything after that point: every r becomes a u and every u becomes an r. Every flipped path ends up at the point one unit left and one unit up from B — so it uses n − 1 steps right and n + 1 steps up. That means illegal paths correspond exactly to

C(2n, n−1)   paths

and the reflection is reversible, so the correspondence is perfect. Therefore the answer is

C(2n, n) − C(2n, n−1)

A little algebra simplifies this to the nth Catalan number:

Cₙ = 1/(n+1) · C(2n, n)

For n = 5:

C(10,5) − C(10,4) = 252 − 210 = 42   and   252/6 = 42 ✓

Catalan numbers turn up all over advanced counting — balanced parentheses, triangulations of a polygon, binary trees. AoPS explores them in Intermediate Counting & Probability.
