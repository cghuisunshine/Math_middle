Exercise 5.4.1
How many ways are there to put 5 balls in 2 boxes if
  1. the balls are distinguishable and the boxes are distinguishable?
  2. the balls are distinguishable but the boxes are not?
  3. the balls are not distinguishable but the boxes are?
  4. the balls are not distinguishable and neither are the boxes?

aDistinguishable balls, distinguishable boxes

Step 1 — Think one ball at a timeLabel the balls A, B, C, D, E and the boxes Box 1 and Box 2. Each ball must go somewhere.
Step 2 — Count choices per ballBall A has 2 choices (Box 1 or Box 2). Ball B also has 2 choices, and so on. The choices don't affect each other.
Step 3 — Multiply2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 25 = 32.
Answer: 32 ways

bDistinguishable balls, identical boxes

Step 1 — What matters now?The boxes look the same, so only which balls end up together matters. Swapping the two boxes gives the same arrangement.
Step 2 — List the possible group sizesSplitting 5 balls into two groups: {5, 0}, {4, 1}, {3, 2}.
Step 3 — Count each case
SplitHow to chooseWays
{5, 0}All balls together1
{4, 1}Pick the 1 ball that is alone: C(5,1)5
{3, 2}Pick the 2 balls in the smaller group: C(5,2)10
Step 4 — Add1 + 5 + 10 = 16.

Check: in part (a) every arrangement is counted twice once we can't tell the boxes apart (swap them), and no arrangement looks the same after swapping — so 32 ÷ 2 = 16. ✓

Answer: 16 ways

cIdentical balls, distinguishable boxes

Step 1 — What matters now?The balls are all the same, so we only track how many balls go into Box 1 (the rest go to Box 2).
Step 2 — List the cases
Box 1012345
Box 2543210
Step 3 — CountBox 1 can hold 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 balls → 6 cases.
Answer: 6 ways

dIdentical balls, identical boxes

Step 1 — What matters now?Nothing is labeled, so we only care about how 5 splits into two numbers, where order doesn't matter.
Step 2 — List the splitsFrom part (c), the pairs (0,5) and (5,0) are now the same, (1,4) and (4,1) are the same, and (2,3) and (3,2) are the same.
Step 3 — Count{5, 0}, {4, 1}, {3, 2} → 3 ways.
Answer: 3 ways

Exercise 5.4.2
How many ways are there to put 5 balls in 3 boxes if
  1. the balls are distinguishable and the boxes are distinguishable?
  2. the balls are distinguishable but the boxes are not?
  3. the balls are not distinguishable but the boxes are?
  4. the balls are not distinguishable and neither are the boxes?
  5. ★ Can you explain why your answer to part (b) is not simply your answer to part (a) divided by 3!?

aDistinguishable balls, distinguishable boxes

Step 1 — One ball at a timeEach of the 5 labeled balls can go into Box 1, Box 2, or Box 3 → 3 choices each.
Step 2 — Multiply3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 35 = 243.
Answer: 243 ways

bDistinguishable balls, identical boxes

Step 1 — What matters?Boxes look the same, so we only care how the balls are grouped. List the possible group sizes (adding to 5, at most 3 groups): {5,0,0}, {4,1,0}, {3,2,0}, {3,1,1}, {2,2,1}.
Step 2 — Count each case
SizesHow to choose the ballsWays
{5,0,0}All together1
{4,1,0}Pick the lone ball: C(5,1)5
{3,2,0}Pick the pair: C(5,2)10
{3,1,1}Pick the group of 3: C(5,3); the other two are each alone10
{2,2,1}Pick the lone ball (5 ways), then split the other 4 into two pairs (3 ways)5 × 3 = 15

Careful with {2,2,1}: C(4,2) = 6 counts each split of 4 balls into two pairs twice, because the two pairs sit in identical boxes. So it is 6 ÷ 2 = 3.

Step 3 — Add1 + 5 + 10 + 10 + 15 = 41.
Answer: 41 ways

cIdentical balls, distinguishable boxes

Step 1 — What matters?Only how many balls go in each box: (Box 1, Box 2, Box 3) with the numbers adding to 5.
Step 2 — Casework on Box 1If Box 1 has k balls, the other 5 − k balls go into Boxes 2 and 3. From Exercise 5.4.1(c), that can be done in (5 − k) + 1 ways.

Ways for Boxes 2 & 3654321
Step 3 — Add6 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 21.
Answer: 21 ways

dIdentical balls, identical boxes

Step 1 — What matters?Only the group sizes, in any order: write 5 as a sum of at most 3 whole numbers.
Step 2 — List them5+0+0, 4+1+0, 3+2+0, 3+1+1, 2+2+1.
Step 3 — Count5 ways — the same size list we used in part (b).
Answer: 5 ways

e★ Why isn’t (b) = (a) ÷ 3! ?

Step 1 — Try it243 ÷ 6 = 40.5 — not even a whole number, so simple division can’t be right.
Step 2 — When does dividing by 3! work?Dividing by 3! = 6 assumes every grouping in (b) shows up exactly 6 times in (a), once for each way to assign the three groups to the three labeled boxes. That only happens when all three boxes hold different contents.
Step 3 — Where it failsTwo empty boxes have the same contents, so swapping them changes nothing. Example: all 5 balls in one box gives only 3 arrangements in (a) (pick which box), not 6.
Step 4 — Check the numbers
Groupings in (b)CountCopies in (a) eachTotal in (a)
No empty box ({3,1,1}, {2,2,1})256150
One empty box ({4,1,0}, {3,2,0})15690
Two empty boxes ({5,0,0})133
Total243 ✓
Answer: the two empty boxes are identical, so the “all in one box” grouping appears only 3 times in (a), not 6.
Exercise 5.4.3
How many ways are there to put 2 balls in n boxes (where n ≥ 2) if
  1. the balls are distinguishable and the boxes are distinguishable?
  2. the balls are distinguishable but the boxes are not?
  3. the balls are not distinguishable but the boxes are?
  4. the balls are not distinguishable and neither are the boxes?

aDistinguishable balls, distinguishable boxes

Step 1 — One ball at a timeBall 1 can go in any of the n boxes; so can Ball 2.
Step 2 — Multiplyn × n = n2.
Quick check (n = 2)22 = 4, matching the 2-box case. ✓
Answer: n2 ways

bDistinguishable balls, identical boxes

Step 1 — Only two possibilitiesBecause the boxes look alike, all that matters is whether the two balls are together or apart.
Step 2 — CountTogether: 1 way. Apart: 1 way (which boxes doesn’t matter). Both are possible since n ≥ 2.
Answer: 2 ways (for every n ≥ 2)

cIdentical balls, distinguishable boxes

Step 1 — Case: same boxChoose the box that gets both balls: n ways.
Step 2 — Case: different boxesBalls are identical, so just choose which 2 boxes get one ball: C(n, 2) = n(n − 1)/2.
Step 3 — Addn + n(n − 1)/2 = n(n + 1)/2.
Quick check (n = 2)2·3/2 = 3: (2,0), (1,1), (0,2). ✓
Answer: n(n + 1)/2 ways

dIdentical balls, identical boxes

Step 1 — Same idea as (b)Nothing is labeled, so only “together” or “apart” matters.
Step 2 — CountTogether: 1 way. Apart: 1 way.
Answer: 2 ways (for every n ≥ 2)
Review Problem 5.10
In the diagram shown in Figure 5.15 (paths only go up and to the right):
  1. How many paths are there from A to B?
  2. How many paths are there from A to C?
  3. How many paths are there from C to B?
  4. How many paths are there from A to B passing through C?

The grid is 5 squares wide and 4 squares tall. C is the grid point 1 step right and 3 steps up from A (the top-left corner of the square labeled C).

aPaths from A to B

Step 1 — Count the stepsFrom A to B we need 5 Right (R) and 4 Up (U) steps: 9 steps in total.
Step 2 — A path is a wordEach path is an arrangement of RRRRRUUUU (e.g. RRUURURUR), and each arrangement is a different path.
Step 3 — Choose the Up positionsChoose which 4 of the 9 steps are Up: C(9, 4) = (9·8·7·6)/(4·3·2·1) = 126.
Answer: 126 paths

bPaths from A to C

Step 1 — Count the stepsA to C: 1 Right and 3 Up → 4 steps.
Step 2 — ChooseChoose which of the 4 steps is the Right step: C(4, 1) = 4.
Answer: 4 paths

cPaths from C to B

Step 1 — Count the stepsC to B: 4 Right and 1 Up → 5 steps.
Step 2 — ChooseChoose which of the 5 steps is the Up step: C(5, 1) = 5.
Answer: 5 paths

dPaths from A to B through C

Step 1 — Split the tripEvery such path is a path A → C followed by a path C → B.
Step 2 — MultiplyEach of the 4 first halves pairs with each of the 5 second halves: 4 × 5 = 20.
Check20 is less than the 126 total paths, as it should be. ✓
Answer: 20 paths
Review Problem 5.11
In how many ways can we choose 3 distinct letters of the alphabet, without regard to order, if we must choose 1 vowel (A, E, I, O, or U) and 2 consonants?

✓Solution

Step 1 — Sort the letters26 letters = 5 vowels + 21 consonants.
Step 2 — Choose the vowelC(5, 1) = 5 ways.
Step 3 — Choose 2 consonantsOrder doesn’t matter: C(21, 2) = (21 × 20)/2 = 210 ways.
Step 4 — MultiplyThe two choices are independent: 5 × 210 = 1050.

Common mistake: 21 × 20 = 420 counts each consonant pair twice (B-C and C-B), so divide by 2.

Answer: 1050 ways
