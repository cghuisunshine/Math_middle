4.2.1

(a)In how many ways can I choose 4 different officers from a club of 9 people?

(b)In how many ways can I choose a 4-person committee from a club of 9 people?

  1. (a) Officers are distinct roles, so president-then-treasurer is a different outcome from treasurer-then-president. Order matters — this is a permutation.
    9 × 8 × 7 × 6 = 3,024
  2. (b) A committee has no roles. The same four people can be listed in 4! = 24 orders, and the count above treats each of those as separate.
  3. Divide the ordered count by 4! to collapse each group down to one.
    3,024 ÷ 24 = 126   =   C(9,4)
Answer (a)3,024
Answer (b)126
4.2.2

My club has 25 members. In how many ways can I choose members to form a 4-person executive committee?

  1. A committee is unordered, so this is C(25,4).
  2. Count the ordered choices first: 25 people for the first seat, then 24, 23, 22.
    25 × 24 × 23 × 22 = 303,600
  3. Each committee was counted 4! = 24 times, once per arrangement. Divide it out.
    303,600 ÷ 24 = 12,650
Answer12,650
4.2.3

Our water polo team has 15 members. I want to choose a starting lineup consisting of 7 players, one of whom will be the goalie (the other six positions are interchangeable). In how many ways can I choose my starting lineup?

  1. Split the lineup into its two kinds of slots. The goalie is a distinct role; the other six are not.
  2. Pick the goalie first — any of the 15 players.
    15 choices
  3. From the 14 players left, pick 6 field players. Order among them does not matter.
    C(14,6) = (14×13×12×11×10×9) ÷ 6! = 2,162,160 ÷ 720 = 3,003
  4. The two choices are independent, so multiply.
    15 × 3,003 = 45,045
Answer45,045

Choosing the goalie first is what keeps this clean. Choosing all 7 first and then naming a goalie gives C(15,7) × 7 = 6,435 × 7 = 45,045 — the same number, as it must.

4.2.4

Consider a regular octagon. How many triangles can be formed whose vertices are the vertices of the octagon?

  1. A triangle is determined by its three corners, and listing those corners in a different order gives the same triangle. So this is a combination, not a permutation.
  2. No three vertices of a regular octagon lie on a line, so every choice of 3 really does form a triangle — nothing to subtract.
  3. Compute C(8,3).
    C(8,3) = (8 × 7 × 6) ÷ 3! = 336 ÷ 6 = 56
Answer56
4.2.5

The Senate of Aopslandia has 100 members, consisting of 55 members of the Red Party and 45 members of the Blue Party. In how many ways can I choose a 5-person committee consisting of 3 Reds and 2 Blues?

  1. The party quota splits the problem in two. The Reds chosen have no effect on which Blues are available, so handle each pool on its own and multiply at the end.
  2. Choose 3 of the 55 Reds.
    C(55,3) = (55 × 54 × 53) ÷ 6 = 157,410 ÷ 6 = 26,235
  3. Choose 2 of the 45 Blues.
    C(45,2) = (45 × 44) ÷ 2 = 1,980 ÷ 2 = 990
  4. Multiply the two independent counts.
    26,235 × 990 = 25,972,650
Answer25,972,650
4.2.6

My state's lottery has 30 white balls numbered from 1 through 30, and 20 red balls numbered from 1 through 20. In each lottery drawing, 3 of the white balls and 2 of the red balls are drawn. To win, you must match all 3 white balls and both red balls, without regard to the order in which they were drawn. How many possible different combinations may be drawn?

  1. Order is explicitly ignored, so each colour is a combination.
  2. Choose 3 white balls of 30.
    C(30,3) = (30 × 29 × 28) ÷ 6 = 24,360 ÷ 6 = 4,060
  3. Choose 2 red balls of 20.
    C(20,2) = (20 × 19) ÷ 2 = 380 ÷ 2 = 190
  4. Multiply — every white triple can pair with every red pair.
    4,060 × 190 = 771,400
Answer771,400

A single ticket wins with probability 1 in 771,400 — considerably better odds than the 1-in-12-million lottery worked through in Problem 4.3.
