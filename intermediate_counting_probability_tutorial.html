Lesson 1 Foundations

Review Toolkit

Reconnect constructive counting, probability, expected value, Pascal's triangle, and summation notation.

Key idea

Start by identifying whether the problem is a sequence of choices, an unordered selection, or a probability question. The core tools are the multiplication principle, combinations, complements, and expected value.

\[\binom nr=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!},\qquad P(E)=\frac{\text{favorable}}{\text{total}}\]

Proof

The combination formula follows by first arranging (r) chosen objects in (P(n,r)=n!/(n-r)!) ways, then dividing by (r!) because each unordered group was counted once for every possible internal order.

Worked example

How many six-digit strings contain exactly two zeros?

Choose the two zero positions, then fill the other four positions with nonzero digits:

\[\binom62\cdot 9^4=98{,}415.\]

Common mistake

Do not decide on a formula from a keyword alone. “Choose” can still involve order, and favorable-over-total probability works directly only when the outcomes are equally likely.

Practice

Five fair coins are tossed. What is the probability of at least one head?

Show answer

Use the complement: no heads means all five coins are tails.

\[1-\left(\frac12\right)^5=\frac{31}{32}.\]
Lesson 2 Language of proof

Sets and Logic

Use precise set operations and valid logical implications to organize a counting argument.

Key idea

Sets organize collections of outcomes, while logic controls the validity of proofs. Important operations are union, intersection, complement, and the power set.

\[|\mathcal P(S)|=2^{|S|}.\]

Proof

If (S) has (n) elements, construct a subset by making one yes-or-no decision for each element. The multiplication principle gives (2^n) subsets. This is also a bijection between subsets and binary strings of length (n).

Worked example

If (S=\{1,2,3,4\}), then (S) has (2^4=16) subsets. Removing (S) itself leaves 15 proper subsets.

Common mistake

An implication and its converse are not equivalent. From “if (P), then (Q),” you may infer the contrapositive “if not (Q), then not (P),” but not automatically “if (Q), then (P).”

Practice

A set has 7 elements. How many of its subsets contain a particular element (a)?

Show answer

Include (a), then independently decide whether to include each of the other 6 elements.

\[2^6=64.\]
Lesson 3 Correcting overlap

Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE)

Count a union by alternating additions and subtractions of intersections.

Key idea

Inclusion-exclusion counts a union by correcting overlap. Add individual sets, subtract pairwise intersections, add triple intersections, and continue with alternating signs.

\[|A\cup B|=|A|+|B|-|A\cap B|.\]

Proof

An object lying in exactly (j) sets receives total weight

\[\binom j1-\binom j2+\binom j3-\cdots+(-1)^{j+1}\binom jj=1.\]

Thus every object in the union is counted exactly once.

Worked example

From 1 through 100, count integers divisible by 2, 3, or 5:

\[50+33+20-16-10-6+3=74.\]

The subtractions use multiples of 6, 10, and 15; the final addition restores multiples of 30.

Common mistake

Do not stop after subtracting pairwise overlaps in a three-set problem. Items in all three sets were then counted zero times, so the triple intersection must be added back.

Practice

How many integers from 1 through 60 are divisible by 4 or 6?

Show answer

The overlap consists of multiples of (lcm(4,6)=12).

\[15+10-5=20.\]
Lesson 4 Count by building or matching

Constructive Counting and Bijections

Describe every object exactly once, or match a hard set to an easy set.

Key idea

Constructive counting builds each object through a sequence of legal choices. A bijection instead matches every object in a difficult set with exactly one object in an easier set.

Proof

If a map from (A) to (B) is one-to-one and onto, then each element of (B) is paired with exactly one element of (A). Counting either side therefore counts the pairs, so (|A|=|B|).

Worked example

Count the even-sized subsets of ({1,2,...,n}). Toggle whether (n) is present. This reversible operation pairs every even subset with one odd subset. Since there are (2^n) subsets total, the answer is

\[2^{n-1}.\]

Common mistake

Showing how to send each object in (A) to an object in (B) is not enough. You must also show uniqueness and that every object in (B) is reached.

Practice

Use a bijection to count subsets of an (n)-element set that contain a fixed element (x).

Show answer

Delete (x) from each such subset. This gives a bijection with all subsets of the other (n-1) elements.

\[2^{n-1}.\]
Lesson 5 Force a collision

Pigeonhole Principle

More objects than boxes guarantees that some box receives multiple objects.

Key idea

Placing (n) objects into (k) boxes forces some box to contain at least

\[\left\lceil\frac nk\right\rceil\]

objects.

Proof

If every box contained at most (ceil(n/k) - 1) objects, the total would be less than (n), contradicting the fact that all (n) objects were placed.

Worked example

Among 25 people, some birth month contains at least

\[\left\lceil\frac{25}{12}\right\rceil=3\]

birthdays. People are the pigeons and months are the holes.

Common mistake

Do not merely say “by pigeonhole.” Name the pigeons, name the holes, and explain what two pigeons sharing a hole means in the original problem.

Practice

Show that among any 11 integers, two have the same remainder when divided by 10.

Show answer

The 11 integers are pigeons and the 10 possible remainders (0,1,...,9) are holes. Two integers must occupy the same remainder class.

Lesson 6 Add local contributions

Constructive Expectation

Break a complicated random variable into indicators and add their expectations.

Key idea

Write a complicated random variable as a sum of simple indicator variables. Linearity of expectation then gives

\[E(X_1+\cdots+X_m)=E(X_1)+\cdots+E(X_m).\]

Proof

Expectation is a weighted average. Distributing the sum inside that average separates the contribution of each variable. Independence is not required.

Worked example

Randomly permute (1,2,...,n). Let (I_i=1) if (i) remains fixed. Since (P(I_i=1)=1/n),

\[E(I_1+\cdots+I_n)=n\cdot\frac1n=1.\]

Common mistake

Do not multiply probabilities or assume independence when you only need an expected total. Indicators may be dependent and linearity still works.

Practice

What is the expected number of heads in 20 fair coin tosses?

Show answer

Use one indicator for each toss. Every indicator has expectation (1/2).

\[20\cdot\frac12=10.\]
Lesson 7 Identical objects, labeled boxes

Distributions (Stars and Bars)

Encode a distribution with stars for objects and bars for separators.

Key idea

Stars and bars counts ways to place indistinguishable objects into distinguishable boxes.

\[x_1+\cdots+x_k=n,\ x_i\ge0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \binom{n+k-1}{k-1}.\]

Proof

Represent the (n) objects by stars and use (k-1) bars to separate the (k) boxes. Every distribution corresponds to one ordering of (n) stars and (k-1) bars, so choose the bar positions.

Worked example

Distribute 12 identical candies among 4 children, with at least 2 each. Give each child 2 first; 4 candies remain:

\[y_1+y_2+y_3+y_4=4\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\binom73=35.\]

Common mistake

Stars and bars does not apply unchanged when the objects are distinct, the boxes are indistinguishable, or upper bounds are present.

Practice

How many nonnegative integer solutions satisfy (a+b+c=8)?

Show answer

Arrange 8 stars and 2 bars.

\[\binom{8+3-1}{3-1}=\binom{10}{2}=45.\]
Lesson 8 Prove an infinite chain

Mathematical Induction

Establish a base case, then prove that each true case forces the next.

Key idea

Induction proves a statement for all integers by establishing a base case and showing that (P(k)) forces (P(k+1)).

Proof

For (1+2+\cdots+n=n(n+1)/2), the case (n=1) is true. Assuming the formula for (k),

\[\frac{k(k+1)}2+(k+1)=\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}2,\]

which is the formula for (k+1).

Worked example

Prove (1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2). Adding the next odd number gives

\[k^2+(2k+1)=(k+1)^2.\]

Common mistake

The induction hypothesis is an assumption for an arbitrary (k), not proof that the statement is true. It must be used to derive the next case.

Practice

Use induction to prove (1+2+4+\cdots+2^n=2^{n+1}-1).

Show answer

The base case (n=0) is (1=2-1). Assuming the result through (2^k), add (2^{k+1}):

\[(2^{k+1}-1)+2^{k+1}=2^{k+2}-1.\]
Lesson 9 Count by the last move

Fibonacci Numbers

A two-case decomposition often produces the Fibonacci recurrence.

Key idea

The Fibonacci sequence satisfies (F_0=0), (F_1=1), and

\[F_n=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}.\]

It appears when a final choice removes either one unit or two units.

Proof

A tiling of a length-(n) board by squares and dominoes ends in exactly one of two ways: a square after a tiling of length (n-1), or a domino after a tiling of length (n-2). These cases are disjoint and exhaustive.

Worked example

Let (T_n) be the number of such tilings. Since (T_0=T_1=1), we get (T_n=F_{n+1}). A board of length 6 therefore has

\[T_6=F_7=13\]

tilings.

Common mistake

A recurrence does not determine a sequence without enough initial values. Different base cases can produce entirely different sequences with the same recurrence.

Practice

How many binary strings of length 5 contain no consecutive 1s?

Show answer

Split by the last digit. A string ending in 0 follows any valid length-4 string; a string ending in 1 must end in 01 after a valid length-3 string. Thus the same Fibonacci recurrence gives

\[F_7=13.\]
Lesson 10 Break objects into smaller objects

Recursion and Catalan Numbers

Define a count from earlier counts, then solve or exploit the recurrence.

Key idea

Recursion breaks an object into smaller objects of the same type. Catalan numbers satisfy

\[C_0=1,\qquad C_{n+1}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}C_iC_{n-i}.\]

Proof

For balanced parentheses, match the first opening parenthesis with its closing partner. If (i) pairs lie inside, then (n-i) pairs lie after it, giving (C_iC_{n-i}) objects in that case. Sum over (i).

Worked example

The closed formula is

\[C_n=\frac1{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}.\]

For (n=4), (C_4=\binom84/5=14).

Common mistake

Do not confuse a recurrence with a closed formula. A recurrence explains how objects decompose; a closed formula gives a direct value. Each requires its own justification.

Practice

How many balanced parenthesis strings contain exactly 3 pairs of parentheses?

Show answer
\[C_3=\frac14\binom63=5.\]
Lesson 11 Update the sample space

Conditional Probability

Once a condition is known, discard outcomes that violate it and renormalize.

Key idea

Conditioning replaces the original sample space with the outcomes satisfying the condition.

\[P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.\]

Proof

Within event (B), the successful part is (A ∩ B). Dividing both probabilities by the total probability of (B) renormalizes the restricted sample space to probability 1.

Worked example

Two cards are drawn without replacement. Given that the first is an ace, 3 aces remain among 51 cards:

\[P(\text{second ace}\mid\text{first ace})=\frac3{51}=\frac1{17}.\]

Common mistake

(P(A | B)) and (P(B | A)) are generally different. Identify the condition first, then rebuild the sample space around it.

Practice

An integer is chosen uniformly from 1 through 20. Given that it is prime, what is the probability it is even?

Show answer

The primes are (2,3,5,7,11,13,17,19). Only 2 is even.

\[\frac18.\]
Lesson 12 Count the same set twice

Combinatorial Identities

Prove an algebraic identity by showing both sides count the same objects.

Key idea

A combinatorial identity can be proved by counting the same set in two different ways. Committee selection and lattice paths are especially useful models.

Proof

Vandermonde's identity counts an (r)-person committee chosen from groups of sizes (m) and (n):

\[\sum_k\binom mk\binom n{r-k}=\binom{m+n}{r}.\]

The left side classifies committees by how many members come from the first group.

Worked example

Every subset of an (n)-element set has some size (k). Counting all subsets by size gives

\[\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk=2^n.\]

Common mistake

Do not manipulate factorials without tracking the valid index range. Terms such as (C nr) are zero when (r<0) or (r>n), which often resolves boundary cases.

Practice

Prove the hockey-stick identity (Σ_{k=r}^{n}\binom kr=\binom{n+1}{r+1}).

Show answer

Choose (r+1) numbers from (1,2,...,n+1) and classify the choice by its largest number (k+1). The remaining (r) numbers can be chosen in (C kr) ways.

Lesson 13 Make time and position visible

Events With States

Represent a process by its current state, then attach transitions, probabilities, or winning labels.

Key idea

A state records all information from the past that can affect the future. Directed edges represent legal transitions, with probabilities on random transitions.

Proof

If (E_s) is the expected remaining time from state (s), first-step analysis gives

\[E_s=1+\sum_t p(s,t)E_t,\]

with (E_s=0) at terminal states.

Worked example

Toss a fair coin until the first head. From the starting state, one toss is used; with probability (1/2) a tail returns to the same state:

\[E=1+\frac12E\quad\Longrightarrow\quad E=2.\]

Common mistake

A state that forgets relevant history is not a valid state. If the future probabilities depend on information you discarded, expand the state description.

Practice

Roll a fair die repeatedly until the first 6. What is the expected number of rolls?

Show answer

First-step analysis gives

\[E=1+\frac56E\quad\Longrightarrow\quad E=6.\]
Lesson 14 Store counts in coefficients

Generating Functions

Translate a counting problem into algebra with a formal power series.

Key idea

A generating function stores a sequence in its coefficients:

\[A(x)=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+\cdots.\]

The coefficient of (x^n) records the count for size (n).

Proof

When (A(x)) and (B(x)) are multiplied, the coefficient of (x^n) is

\[[x^n]A(x)B(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_kb_{n-k}.\]

This is exactly the casework obtained by splitting a total size (n) between two independent components.

Worked example

Red tokens may occur any number of times and blue tokens an even number of times. Their product is

\[\frac1{1-x}\cdot\frac1{1-x^2}.\]

For total size 6, the blue count can be (0,2,4,6), so the coefficient of (x^6) is 4.

Common mistake

In combinatorics, generating functions are often formal power series. Do not reject a valid coefficient argument merely because the infinite series does not converge at a particular numerical value of (x).

Practice

Find the coefficient of (x^5) in (1/(1-x)^3).

Show answer

The coefficient counts nonnegative solutions to (a+b+c=5).

\[\binom{5+3-1}{3-1}=\binom72=21.\]
Lesson 15 Count relationships

Graph Theory

Model objects as vertices and pairwise relationships as edges.

Key idea

A graph models objects as vertices and pairwise relationships as edges. Degree, connectivity, cycles, trees, bipartiteness, and planarity translate structural questions into counts.

Proof

Every edge contributes 1 to the degree of each endpoint, so it contributes 2 to the total degree sum:

\[\sum_v\deg(v)=2E.\]

This is the handshake lemma.

Worked example

A graph has degrees (3,3,2,2,2,2). The degree sum is 14, so

\[E=\frac{14}{2}=7.\]

Common mistake

Eulerian and Hamiltonian describe different goals. Eulerian paths use every edge; Hamiltonian paths visit every vertex. Criteria for one do not automatically apply to the other.

Practice

A connected planar graph has 10 vertices and 15 edges. How many faces does a planar drawing have?

Show answer

Use Euler's formula (V-E+F=2).

\[F=2-10+15=7.\]
Lesson 16 Choosing the tool

Synthesis and Challenge Strategy

Advanced problems often combine two or more methods.

Key idea

Define the counted object first. Then try construction, complement, inclusion-exclusion, bijection, recurrence, indicators, conditioning, states, identities, or generating functions in that order of need.

Proof

Changing representations is valid when the transformation preserves exactly the objects being counted. A bijection preserves cardinality; disjoint casework preserves totals; a recurrence preserves a decomposition into smaller objects.

Worked example

Count nonnegative solutions to (x+y+z=12) with (x ≤ 4). Count all solutions, then subtract those with (x ≥ 5):

\[\binom{14}{2}-\binom92=91-36=55.\]

Common mistake

Avoid “formula shopping.” If you cannot explain what each factor, term, or subtraction counts, the expression is not yet a solution.

Practice

Prove (Σ_{k=0}^{n}k\binom nk=n2^{n-1}) by counting committees with a captain.

Show answer

Choose the captain first in (n) ways, then choose any subset of the remaining (n-1) people: (n2^{n-1}). Alternatively, choose a (k)-person committee and then one of its (k) members as captain, giving the sum on the left.

Method Selection Map

When a problem resists the first approach, change the representation before adding more algebra.

Ask these questions

  1. What exactly is one outcome? Define the counted object or state.
  2. Can I construct it uniquely? Handle the tightest restriction first.
  3. Is there overlap? Try a complement or inclusion-exclusion.
  4. Can I match it to something easier? Search for a bijection.
  5. Does the last move split the problem? Write a recurrence.
  6. Is randomness involved? Try indicators, conditioning, or state equations.

Mixed Practice

Name the method before computing. The method choice is part of the solution.

Problems

  1. Count nonnegative solutions to \(x+y+z=12\) with \(x\le 4\).
  2. Show that among 20 integers, two differ by a multiple of 19.
  3. Find the expected number of fixed points in a random permutation of 12 objects.
  4. Prove \(\sum_{k=0}^n k\binom nk=n2^{n-1}\) by double counting.
  5. Find the number of length-8 binary strings with no consecutive 1s.
  6. A connected planar graph has 10 vertices and 15 edges. How many faces does it have?
Show all answers
  1. All solutions: \(\binom{14}2=91\). Subtract \(x\ge5\): \(\binom92=36\). Answer: \(55\).
  2. Use the 19 residue classes modulo 19 as holes.
  3. Write the count as 12 fixed-position indicators. Each has expectation \(1/12\), so the answer is \(1\).
  4. Choose a captain and any subset of the other \(n-1\) people, or first choose a size-\(k\) committee and then its captain.
  5. The count follows the Fibonacci recurrence and equals \(F_{10}=55\).
  6. Euler's formula gives \(F=2-V+E=7\).

Final Advice

Advanced counting is less about memorizing more formulas and more about recognizing equivalent descriptions. Build the object, inspect its overlaps, try a reversible correspondence, and only then choose the algebra.
