Lesson 1 Foundations

Review Toolkit

Reconnect constructive counting, probability, expected value, Pascal's triangle, and summation notation.

Core facts

  • Multiplication principle: multiply the choices in a sequence of decisions.
  • Permutations arrange objects; combinations select them without order: C(n,r) = n!/[r!(n-r)!].
  • For equally likely outcomes, P(E) = favorable outcomes / total outcomes.
  • Complement: P(E) = 1 - P(not E). Linearity of expectation: E(X+Y) = E(X)+E(Y).
  • Binomial theorem: (x+y)^n = sum from k=0 to n of C(n,k)x^(n-k)y^k.

Worked example

How many six-digit strings contain exactly two zeros? Choose the two zero positions in C(6,2) ways. Each remaining position has 9 nonzero choices, so the count is C(6,2)9^4 = 98,415. The construction handles the restriction before filling unrestricted positions.

Checkpoint

Five fair coins are tossed. Find the probability of at least one head.

Show answer

Answer: Count the complement. 1 - (1/2)^5 = 31/32.

Lesson 2 Language of proof

Sets and Logic

Use precise set operations and valid logical implications to organize a counting argument.

Sets

  • A union B contains elements in at least one of the sets.
  • A intersection B contains elements in both sets.
  • A complement contains elements in the universal set but not in A.
  • A power set contains every subset; if |S|=n, then |P(S)|=2^n.

Logic that matters

The implication “if P, then Q” has contrapositive “if not Q, then not P”; these are logically equivalent. Its converse “if Q, then P” is a different claim. Quantifiers also matter: “for every x there exists y” does not generally mean “there exists one y that works for every x.”

Checkpoint

Let S={1,2,3,4}. How many proper subsets does S have?

Show answer

Answer: There are 2^4=16 subsets total. Excluding S itself leaves 15 proper subsets.

Lesson 3 Correcting overlap

Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE)

Count a union by alternating additions and subtractions of intersections.

Formulas

  • Two sets: |A union B| = |A| + |B| - |A intersection B|.
  • Three sets: add the three single-set counts, subtract the three pairwise intersections, then add the triple intersection.
  • For many properties, continue alternating signs according to intersection size.

Why the signs alternate

An object lying in exactly j sets is counted C(j,1)-C(j,2)+C(j,3)-...+(-1)^(j+1)C(j,j)=1 time. PIE is therefore not a formula to apply blindly: its job is to make the final multiplicity of every desired object exactly one.

Worked example

From 1 through 100, how many integers are divisible by 2, 3, or 5? Singles give 50+33+20. Subtract multiples of 6, 10, and 15: 16+10+6. Add multiples of 30: 3. The result is 103-32+3=74.

Checkpoint

How many integers from 1 through 60 are divisible by 4 or 6?

Show answer

Answer: 15+10-5=20, because the overlap consists of multiples of lcm(4,6)=12.

Lesson 4 Count by building or matching

Constructive Counting and Bijections

Describe every object exactly once, or match a hard set to an easy set.

Two approaches

  • Constructive counting records the number of legal choices at each stage.
  • A bijection is a reversible one-to-one match between two sets, proving equal cardinality.
  • A proposed correspondence must work uniquely in both directions.

Worked example

Count subsets of {1,2,...,n} with even size. Toggle whether n is present. This maps every even subset to a unique odd subset and reverses itself. Since there are 2^n subsets total, exactly 2^(n-1) have even size.

Partitions and Ferrers diagrams

A partition writes an integer as a sum of positive integers while ignoring order. A Ferrers diagram turns row lengths into dots. Reflecting the diagram across its diagonal gives a bijection between partitions with at most k parts and partitions whose largest part is at most k.

Lesson 5 Force a collision

Pigeonhole Principle

More objects than boxes guarantees that some box receives multiple objects.

General form

Placing n objects into k boxes forces at least one box to contain at least floor((n-1)/k)+1, equivalently ceil(n/k), objects.

Choosing the boxes

The arithmetic is usually easy; the modeling is the hard part. Boxes may be residues, intervals, last digits, subsets, or partial sums. State explicitly what the pigeons and holes are, then state what a shared hole means in the original problem.

Worked example

Among 13 people, at least two share a birth month: the people are pigeons and the 12 months are holes. More strongly, among 25 people, some month contains at least ceil(25/12)=3 birthdays.

Checkpoint

Show that among any 11 integers, two have the same remainder when divided by 10.

Show answer

Answer: There are 10 remainder classes, 0 through 9, and 11 integers.

Lesson 6 Add local contributions

Constructive Expectation

Break a complicated random variable into indicators and add their expectations.

Linearity

If X=X1+X2+...+Xm, then E(X)=E(X1)+...+E(Xm) whether or not the variables are independent. For an indicator Ii of an event, E(Ii)=P(event i).

Worked example

Randomly permute 1,2,...,n. Let Ii=1 when i remains in its original position. Then the number of fixed points is X=I1+...+In. Each position is fixed with probability 1/n, so E(X)=n(1/n)=1.

Existence from expectation

If the average value of a collection is at least a, some member has value at least a. Thus a randomized construction can prove that a deterministic object with a desired property exists, even when it does not identify the object directly.

Lesson 7 Identical objects, labeled boxes

Distributions (Stars and Bars)

Encode a distribution with stars for objects and bars for separators.

Standard counts

  • Positive solutions to x1+...+xk=n: C(n-1,k-1).
  • Nonnegative solutions to x1+...+xk=n: C(n+k-1,k-1).
  • Lower bounds are handled by giving each box its required minimum first.

Worked example

Distribute 12 identical candies among 4 children so each receives at least 2. Give each child 2 first, leaving 4 candies. The remaining nonnegative solutions to y1+y2+y3+y4=4 number C(7,3)=35.

Warning and checkpoint

Stars and bars requires indistinguishable objects and distinguishable boxes. Extra upper bounds may require casework or inclusion-exclusion.

How many nonnegative solutions satisfy a+b+c=8?

Show answer

Answer: C(10,2)=45.

Lesson 8 Prove an infinite chain

Mathematical Induction

Establish a base case, then prove that each true case forces the next.

Structure

  1. State the proposition P(n) precisely.
  2. Prove the base case.
  3. Assume P(k) for an arbitrary k (the induction hypothesis).
  4. Use that assumption to prove P(k+1).

Worked proof

Claim: 1+2+...+n=n(n+1)/2. It holds for n=1. Assume the formula for k. Then 1+...+k+(k+1)=k(k+1)/2+(k+1)=(k+1)(k+2)/2, which is the required formula for k+1.

Strong induction

Strong induction allows the step for P(k+1) to use every earlier case P(1),...,P(k). It is natural when an object can break into pieces of several possible smaller sizes, as in factorization, tilings, and recurrences.

Lesson 9 Count by the last move

Fibonacci Numbers

A two-case decomposition often produces the Fibonacci recurrence.

Sequence

With F0=0 and F1=1, define Fn=F(n-1)+F(n-2). The sequence begins 0,1,1,2,3,5,8,....

Worked example

Let Tn count tilings of a length-n board using squares of length 1 and dominoes of length 2. A tiling ends with either a square, leaving any tiling of length n-1, or a domino, leaving any tiling of length n-2. Thus Tn=T(n-1)+T(n-2), with T0=T1=1, so Tn=F(n+1).

Closed form

Binet's formula is Fn = (phi^n-psi^n)/sqrt(5), where phi=(1+sqrt(5))/2 and psi=(1-sqrt(5))/2. The recurrence is usually better for counting proofs; the closed form is useful for asymptotics and algebraic identities.

Lesson 10 Break objects into smaller objects

Recursion and Catalan Numbers

Define a count from earlier counts, then solve or exploit the recurrence.

Linear recurrences

For a constant-coefficient recurrence, try a solution an=r^n. The resulting characteristic equation gives exponential building blocks. Use initial conditions to determine the constants.

Catalan decomposition

The Catalan numbers satisfy C0=1 and C(n+1)=sum from i=0 to n of Ci*C(n-i). For balanced parentheses, match the first opening parenthesis with its closing partner: the material inside and after it are independent smaller balanced strings.

Formula and checkpoint

Cn = C(2n,n)/(n+1). Catalan numbers count many equivalent families: balanced parentheses, binary trees, polygon triangulations, and monotone paths that do not cross a diagonal.

Checkpoint: C4 = C(8,4)/5 = 14.

Lesson 11 Update the sample space

Conditional Probability

Once a condition is known, discard outcomes that violate it and renormalize.

Formula

P(A given B)=P(A intersection B)/P(B), provided P(B)>0. Equivalently, P(A intersection B)=P(B)P(A given B).

Worked example

Two cards are drawn without replacement. Given that the first card is an ace, the probability the second is also an ace is 3/51=1/17. The condition changes both the number of remaining favorable cards and the size of the sample space.

Bayes and independence

Bayes' rule reverses a condition: P(A given B)=P(B given A)P(A)/P(B). Events A and B are independent exactly when P(A given B)=P(A), or equivalently P(A intersection B)=P(A)P(B).

Lesson 12 Count the same set twice

Combinatorial Identities

Prove an algebraic identity by showing both sides count the same objects.

Three proof styles

  • Algebra: manipulate factorials or known identities.
  • Lattice paths: interpret a binomial coefficient as a path count.
  • Committees: interpret C(n,r) as selecting an r-person group.

Worked identity

Vandermonde's identity is sum over k of C(m,k)C(n,r-k)=C(m+n,r). The right side chooses r people from two groups containing m and n people. On the left, k records how many were chosen from the first group; summing covers every possible composition exactly once.

Useful identities

Symmetry: C(n,r)=C(n,n-r). Pascal: C(n,r)=C(n-1,r-1)+C(n-1,r). Row sum: sum from r=0 to n of C(n,r)=2^n.

Lesson 13 Make time and position visible

Events With States

Represent a process by its current state, then attach transitions, probabilities, or winning labels.

State diagrams

A state stores all information from the past that can affect the future. Directed edges represent legal next moves. For a random walk, transition probabilities on outgoing edges sum to 1.

First-step analysis

Let E_s be the expected remaining time from state s. If one step moves to state t with probability p(s,t), then E_s = 1 + sum_t p(s,t)E_t, with E_s=0 at terminal states. Solve the resulting system of equations.

Strategy games

In a finite two-player game, mark terminal states as winning or losing and work backward. A state is winning if it has at least one move to a losing state; it is losing if every legal move goes to a winning state.

Lesson 14 Store counts in coefficients

Generating Functions

Translate a counting problem into algebra with a formal power series.

Encoding

For counts a0,a1,a2,..., the ordinary generating function is A(x)=a0+a1·x+a2·x^2+.... Multiplying generating functions performs all compatible case combinations at once; the coefficient of x^n selects total size n.

Worked example

Suppose red tokens contribute any nonnegative number and blue tokens contribute an even number. Their generating functions are 1/(1-x) and 1/(1-x^2). The coefficient of x^n in their product counts solutions to r+b=n with b even.

Classic functions

1/(1-x)^k encodes distributions into k labeled boxes. The partition generating function is the infinite product product over j>=1 of 1/(1-x^j). The Fibonacci generating function is x/(1-x-x^2) for F0=0,F1=1.

Lesson 15 Count relationships

Graph Theory

Model objects as vertices and pairwise relationships as edges.

Degree and structure

  • Handshake lemma: sum of all vertex degrees = 2E; hence the number of odd-degree vertices is even.
  • A connected acyclic graph is a tree; a tree with V vertices has V-1 edges.
  • A graph is bipartite exactly when it has no odd cycle.

Planarity

For a connected planar drawing, Euler's formula is V-E+F=2. If a simple connected planar graph has at least three vertices, then E <= 3V-6; violating this inequality proves nonplanarity, though satisfying it does not prove planarity.

Eulerian versus Hamiltonian

An Eulerian circuit uses every edge exactly once and exists in a connected graph when every vertex has even degree. A Hamiltonian cycle visits every vertex exactly once; there is no equally simple universal test.

Checkpoint

A graph has degrees 3,3,2,2,2,2. How many edges?

Show answer

Answer: The degree sum is 14, so E=14/2=7.

Lesson 16 Choosing the tool

Synthesis and Challenge Strategy

Advanced problems often combine two or more methods.

Decision sequence

  1. Define exactly what one counted object or state is.
  2. Try a direct construction; handle the tightest restriction first.
  3. If overlap appears, consider complement or inclusion-exclusion.
  4. If objects are hard to count, search for a bijection or recurrence.
  5. If the problem is random, try indicators, conditioning, or states.
  6. If coefficients or many cases appear, try a combinatorial identity or generating function.

Mixed practice

  1. Count nonnegative solutions to x+y+z=12 with x<=4.
  2. Prove that a graph with six vertices of degree 3 has nine edges.
  3. Find the expected number of heads in 20 fair coin tosses without using the binomial distribution.
  4. Prove sum from k=0 to n of k·C(n,k)=n·2^(n-1) by marking one selected committee member as captain.

Answers

Show all answers
  1. All solutions: C(14,2)=91. Subtract those with x>=5: set x'=x-5, giving C(9,2)=36. Answer: 55.
  2. The degree sum is 18; divide by 2.
  3. Sum 20 indicator variables, each with expectation 1/2; answer: 10.
  4. The right side chooses a captain in n ways and any subset of the remaining n-1 people. The left side first chooses a k-person committee, then its captain, and sums over k.

Method Selection Map

When a problem resists the first approach, change the representation before adding more algebra.

Ask these questions

  1. What exactly is one outcome? Define the counted object or state.
  2. Can I construct it uniquely? Handle the tightest restriction first.
  3. Is there overlap? Try a complement or inclusion-exclusion.
  4. Can I match it to something easier? Search for a bijection.
  5. Does the last move split the problem? Write a recurrence.
  6. Is randomness involved? Try indicators, conditioning, or state equations.

Formula quick reference

PIE Add singles, subtract pairs, add triples, and continue alternating.

Pigeonhole Some box has at least \(\lceil n/k\rceil\) objects.

Stars & bars \(\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}\) nonnegative distributions.

Conditional \(P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)\).

Catalan \(C_n=\frac1{n+1}\binom{2n}n\).

Planar \(V-E+F=2\).

Mixed Practice

Name the method before computing. The method choice is part of the solution.

Problems

  1. Count nonnegative solutions to \(x+y+z=12\) with \(x\le 4\).
  2. Show that among 20 integers, two differ by a multiple of 19.
  3. Find the expected number of fixed points in a random permutation of 12 objects.
  4. Prove \(\sum_{k=0}^n k\binom nk=n2^{n-1}\) by double counting.
  5. Find the number of length-8 binary strings with no consecutive 1s.
  6. A connected planar graph has 10 vertices and 15 edges. How many faces does it have?
Show all answers
  1. All solutions: \(\binom{14}2=91\). Subtract \(x\ge5\): \(\binom92=36\). Answer: \(55\).
  2. Use the 19 residue classes modulo 19 as holes.
  3. Write the count as 12 fixed-position indicators. Each has expectation \(1/12\), so the answer is \(1\).
  4. Choose a captain and any subset of the other \(n-1\) people, or first choose a size-\(k\) committee and then its captain.
  5. The count follows the Fibonacci recurrence and equals \(F_{10}=55\).
  6. Euler's formula gives \(F=2-V+E=7\).

Final Advice

Advanced counting is less about memorizing more formulas and more about recognizing equivalent descriptions. Build the object, inspect its overlaps, try a reversible correspondence, and only then choose the algebra.
