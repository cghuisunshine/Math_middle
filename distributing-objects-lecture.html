Four questions,
one way of thinking

Everything here turns on two yes/no answers: are the objects distinguishable, and are the boxes distinguishable? Work the same four examples the lecture works, and watch what each answer changes.

Example 1 objects distinguishable · boxes distinguishable

Poker hands to three players

How many ways are there to deal five-card poker hands from a 52-card deck to each of three players?

The cards are all different and the players are all different, so both sides are distinguishable. Within a hand the order does not matter — a poker hand is a poker hand — so each deal is a combination.

Deal in sequence. Player one takes 5 of 52. Player two takes 5 of the 47 that remain. Player three takes 5 of the remaining 42.

Watch the factorials cancel

52!5! 47! · 47!5! 42! · 42!5! 37!
= 52!5! 5! 5! 37!

Three hands of five — and a fourth box holding the 37 cards nobody was dealt.

So this case does have a closed formula. With n distinguishable objects and k distinguishable boxes holding n1, n2, …, nk objects:

n! / (n1! n2! ⋯ nk!)

The trap in this one is the leftovers. It looks like three boxes, but 15 cards out of 52 are dealt, so the undealt 37 form a fourth box. If the denominators do not use up all of n, you have missed a box.
Example 2 objects identical · boxes distinguishable

Ten identical tokens into five labelled bins

The tokens carry no identity, so an arrangement is nothing but a list of counts. That is exactly the linear equation model: let xi be the number of tokens in bin i and count the non-negative whole-number solutions.

The linear equation model

Ten identical tokens, four dividers. Where the dividers fall is the only decision being made.

Choosing 10 tokens from 5 types with repetition allowed gives

C(5 + 10 − 1, 10) = C(14, 10) = 14! / (10! · 4!) = 1001

Reading it the other way round is the same number: you are choosing which 4 of the 14 positions in the row hold dividers, C(14,4).

Which number goes where. The 5 is the number of bins and the 10 is the number of tokens, and they are not interchangeable. Writing the equation x1 + x2 + x3 + x4 + x5 = 10 first makes it very hard to swap them by accident.
Example 3 objects distinguishable · boxes identical

Four employees into three identical lockers

Four employees — call them A, B, C, D — share three identical lockers, and each locker holds between zero and three of them. This is the hard case, and it is hard for one reason: the lockers are interchangeable, so there is no formula to reach for. You break it into shapes and count each shape.

Read the constraint before anything else. Each locker takes at most three people, so all four in one locker is not allowed. That leaves three possible shapes.

One shape at a time

Why the 2-and-2 shape gets halved

Splitting four people into two pairs looks like C(4,2) = 6: choose who goes in the first locker and the rest follow. But the lockers are identical, so putting AB in one and CD in the other is the same arrangement as putting CD in one and AB in the other. Every split has been counted twice.

C(4,2) = 6, but only 3 are different

Each card shows locker one | locker two. Since the lockers are identical, reading a card backwards gives the same arrangement.

Two mistakes worth naming. First, don't halve the 2-1-1 shape. Once you have chosen the pair with C(4,2), the two people left over are each alone, and there is nothing left to arrange — the lockers are identical, so there is no choice about which single goes where. Second, don't try to permute the lockers at the end. Any instinct to multiply by 3! belongs to labelled boxes, not these.
Example 4 objects identical · boxes identical

Five copies of a book into three identical boxes

Nothing has a label here, which makes this deceptively simple. The books are interchangeable and so are the boxes, so an arrangement is only a list of sizes with no order to it. List them systematically, largest part first, and stop when you run out.

List the cases

5 ways

The temptation is to keep going — to say that 5-0-0 and 0-5-0 and 0-0-5 are three different answers. They are not. The boxes are identical, so there is no first box to speak of. Same books, same boxes, same arrangement.

The formulas are a starting point, not a shortcut. A constraint like "each box holds at most one ball" or "each locker takes at most three" can knock out cases or change the count entirely. Read the restriction first, then decide whether a formula still applies.

Practice: one of each type

Five balls go into seven boxes, and each box holds at most one ball. That restriction is doing a lot of work — try each part before opening the answer.

Five balls, seven boxes, at most one each

(a) Both the balls and the boxes are labelled.

Ball one has 7 boxes to choose from. Ball two has 6 left, ball three has 5, and so on, because no box can take a second ball. That is 7 · 6 · 5 · 4 · 3 = P(7,5) = 2520.

(b) The balls are labelled, the boxes are not.

Every ball ends up alone in some box, and the boxes are interchangeable, so there is nothing to distinguish one outcome from another. Whichever box ball one sits in, that box is identical to every other. 1 way.

(c) The balls are not labelled, the boxes are.

The only decision is which five of the seven boxes end up holding a ball. C(7,5) = 21.

(d) Neither is labelled.

Same reasoning as (b). Once the boxes are unlabelled, whether the balls are labelled changes nothing — five boxes hold one ball each and two are empty. 1 way.

Notice what (a) is not. Without the one-ball limit, labelled-into-labelled would be 75 = 16,807. The restriction turns it into a permutation instead. This is exactly why reaching straight for a formula is risky.
