Question 1one row of 10

A small movie theater has a single row of 10 seats. Six people take seats at random. Two friends arrive next and want to sit next to each other. What is the probability that there are 2 adjacent empty seats for them?

  • A1/2
  • B2/3
  • C5/6
  • D6/7
  • E13/14
Show the worked solution
  1. Count empty seats, not people. Ten seats hold six people, so exactly 4 stay empty, and every set of 4 empty seats is equally likely.
    C(10,4) = 210 equally likely outcomes
  2. Flip to the complement. The friends are stuck exactly when no two empty seats are adjacent, so count those seatings.
  3. Use the gap trick. Put the 6 occupied seats in a line. They create 7 gaps — one before, one after, and five between. Dropping the 4 empty seats into 4 different gaps guarantees no two are neighbours, and every non-adjacent arrangement arises exactly once this way.
    C(7,4) = 35 bad seatings in general: k non-adjacent seats in a row of n → C(n−k+1, k)
  4. Subtract from 1.
    1 − 35/210 = 175/210 = 5/6

Answer (C) 5/6

That gap formula is the engine inside every other problem here. In a row of 3 it gives C(2,2) = 1 — the two outer seats, and nothing else.

Question 23 rows × 3 seats

An airport shuttle has 3 rows of seats with 3 seats in each row. Five passengers have boarded and are distributed randomly among the seats. A couple is next to board. What is the probability there will be 2 adjacent seats in the same row for the couple?

  • A5/21
  • B3/7
  • C2/3
  • D16/21
  • E6/7
Show the worked solution
  1. Find the empty seats. Nine seats, five passengers, so 4 seats stay empty.
    C(9,4) = 126 equally likely outcomes
  2. Count the complement — no row holds two adjacent empty seats. Inside one row of 3 the legal empty-sets are: none, any single seat, or the two outer seats.
    Empties in a row0123
    Legal patterns1310
  3. Split 4 empties across 3 rows, with no row taking more than 2.
    2+2+0 : 3 × 1 × 1 = 3 2+1+1 : 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 total = 30 bad seatings
  4. Subtract from 1.
    1 − 30/126 = 96/126 = 16/21

Answer (D) 16/21

Fewer rows than the original, but the row table is identical. Only the case list changes.

Question 34 rows × 3 seats

A small airplane has 4 rows of seats with 3 seats in each row. Seven passengers have boarded the plane and are distributed randomly among the seats. A married couple is next to board. What is the probability there will be 2 adjacent seats in the same row for the couple?

  • A2/11
  • B7/11
  • C3/4
  • D9/11
  • E10/11
Show the worked solution
  1. Find the empty seats. Twelve seats, seven passengers, so 5 stay empty.
    C(12,5) = 792 equally likely outcomes
  2. Same row table as the original — a row of 3 can hold 0, 1, or 2 empties legally (2 only as the outer pair), never 3.
  3. Spread 5 empties over 4 rows, at most 2 per row.
    2+2+1+0 : C(4,2) × 2 × 3 = 6 × 2 × 3 = 36 2+1+1+1 : 4 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 108 total = 144 bad seatings
    In the first case, pick the two rows holding a pair, then which of the two remaining rows holds the single empty seat, then which of its 3 seats that is.
  4. Subtract from 1.
    1 − 144/792 = 648/792 = 9/11

Answer (D) 9/11

One extra empty seat pushed the answer from 20/33 up to 9/11. With 5 empties among 4 rows, some row is forced to hold two.

Question 43 rows × 4 seats

A tour bus has 3 rows of seats with 4 seats in each row. Eight passengers have boarded and are distributed randomly among the seats. Two friends are next to board. What is the probability there will be 2 adjacent seats in the same row for them?

  • A19/55
  • B6/11
  • C36/55
  • D41/55
  • E4/5
Show the worked solution
  1. Find the empty seats. Twelve seats, eight passengers, so 4 stay empty.
    C(12,4) = 495 equally likely outcomes
  2. Rebuild the row table for width 4. This is the only thing that really changes. Two non-touching empties in a row of 4 can be seats 1&3, 1&4, or 2&4 — that is C(4−2+1, 2) = C(3,2) = 3. Three empties always leave a touching pair.
    Empties in a row01234
    Legal patterns14300
  3. Split 4 empties across 3 rows, at most 2 per row.
    2+2+0 : 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 2+1+1 : 3 × 3 × 4 × 4 = 144 total = 171 bad seatings
    In the second case: choose the row holding a pair (3), the pattern inside it (3), then a seat in each of the other two rows (4 × 4).
  4. Subtract from 1.
    1 − 171/495 = 324/495 = 36/55

Answer (C) 36/55

Do this one slowly. Everything downstream is just the row table, so if the 3 is wrong, all the case work is wrong too.

Question 54 rows × 3 seats

A small airplane has 4 rows of seats with 3 seats in each row. Six passengers have boarded the plane and are distributed randomly among the seats. Three friends are next to board, and they want a full row to themselves. What is the probability that some row is completely empty?

  • A2/7
  • B1/3
  • C5/14
  • D3/7
  • E1/2
Show the worked solution
  1. Find the empty seats. Twelve seats, six passengers, so 6 stay empty.
    C(12,6) = 924 equally likely outcomes
  2. Count the good ones directly this time. The complement — "no row is fully empty" — is messier here, but "row i is fully empty" is easy to count one row at a time.
  3. One named row empty. That row uses 3 of the 6 empty seats; the other 3 empties sit anywhere among the remaining 9 seats.
    C(9,3) = 84 per row 4 rows × 84 = 336
  4. Find the double counting. Two empty rows use all 6 empty seats at once, so such a seating shows up once for each of its two empty rows. Three empty rows would need 9 empties — impossible.
    C(4,2) = 6 seatings with two empty rows
  5. Subtract one extra copy each, not both.
    336 − 6 = 330 330/924 = 5/14

Answer (C) 5/14

Same correction as the 3-by-3 grid problem: a seating counted twice needs one copy removed, not two. Removing both would erase the six best seatings on the plane.
